Discipleship

DNA: Discover, Nurture, Act

Brennan Cattani  ·  September 4, 2026

← Back to Blog

Anyone Who Wants to Be Like Jesus Runs Into the Same Wall

The word Christian means, roughly, "little Christ" — someone who is learning to walk in the ways of Jesus. Which sounds wonderful right up until you take it seriously. Because anyone who actually wants to become more like Him is confronted, fairly quickly, with a harsh reality: a great many things need to change.

Change itself is not a foreign concept to us. We change jobs, habits, diets, cities. But this kind of change raises harder questions. Why should I change? What actually needs to change? How do I go about it? And why, when I genuinely want to, is it so difficult?

A DNA group is our answer to those questions — not as a theory, but as a rhythm. It is the smallest and, we would argue, the most formative space in the life of our church.

What a DNA Group Actually Is

Three people. Men with men, women with women. Meeting weekly.

That is genuinely the whole structure. Three is small enough that nobody can drift to the edge of the room and listen politely. Everybody talks. Everybody is known. And keeping the groups same-sex makes a kind of transparency possible that mixed company rarely does — you can say the true thing.

This is not an accident of scheduling. We are made to relate differently at different scales, and each scale does something the others cannot:

  • Intimate — 2 to 4 people. This is DNA.
  • Personal — 8 to 15 people. This is the core of a missional community.
  • Social — 20 to 50. MC celebrations, serving events.
  • Public — a crowd. Sunday gathering.

You need all four. But there are things that will only ever surface in a room of three. A sermon cannot ask you what you did on Thursday night. A group of forty cannot notice that you have gone quiet for a month.

And to be clear about the goal: it is not to hang out, and it is not even to build friendships — though we hope both happen. The goal is to challenge one another to "grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ" (2 Peter 3:18). Real discipleship is life-on-life. It has never been possible to do it alone.

Head, Heart, Hands

DNA is an acronym, and it names the three movements a group walks through every single week:

  • Discover — teaching the head. What is true?
  • Nurture — shepherding the heart. What am I actually believing?
  • Act — empowering the hands. So what will I do?

The order matters, and so does the fact that it is all three. A group that only ever discovers becomes a study club with excellent notes and unchanged lives. A group that only ever nurtures becomes an endless loop of confession with no traction. A group that only ever acts becomes a to-do list with a Bible verse stapled to it.

None of these can be done by willpower. Each one depends entirely on the Holy Spirit, which is why praying throughout — not just at the end — is not a nicety. It is the mechanism.

Discover — The Head

"If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free."

— John 8:31–32 (ESV)

The group picks a passage and reads it through the week — on their own, ideally with a journal — then comes together having asked the same four questions of it. Every time:

  1. Who is God? What does this text say about His character and nature — holy, eternal, all-powerful, unchanging, compassionate, patient, good? In the stories, watch for what is implied as much as what is stated.
  2. What has He done? What does it say about His work? Jesus is revealed on every page of the Bible (Luke 24:27) — through foreshadowing, through prophecy, through the story itself.
  3. Who are we in light of that? What does it say about our identity? God has always been at work forming "a people for his own possession" (1 Peter 2:9).
  4. How do we live in light of that? Only now. Not first — last.

That final instruction is the hinge, and it is where most Bible study quietly goes wrong. The world hands us a paradigm so early that we stop noticing it: I am what I do. Our doing equals our being. Read Scripture through that lens and the Bible becomes a book about us and our performance — a manual for works righteousness with God somewhere off in the margins.

But the gospel does not move in that direction. It starts with God — who He is and what He has done — and only then arrives at who we are as a result, and finally at how we live. The indicatives lead to the imperatives. The indicatives are what is true no matter what we do. The imperatives are what we do because of what is true. Reverse them and you get religion.

Which is also why Discover is not an information exercise. It is about relationship and transformation. Jesus said the Spirit "will teach you all things" (John 14:26) and "will guide you into all the truth… He will glorify me" (John 16:13–14). His death made it possible for that Spirit to live in us. Your teacher is already in the room.

So each person comes to the meeting ready to answer three things:

  • What did you discover this week? What did you learn, or get reminded of?
  • Where did the Spirit lead you to repentance and faith?
  • How have you struggled with sin and unbelief this week?

Nurture — The Heart

The word heart appears more than 850 times in Scripture, and it means far more than feelings. It is the centre of a person — mind, emotions, and will together. "With the heart a person believes" (Romans 10:10).

Nurture exists because there is often a gap between what we know and what we actually believe. You can hold a doctrine of God's sovereignty in your head and still lie awake at 2am. When we worry, we are placing our hope in something other than God — believing, right at that moment, that something else is in control.

"Each tree is known by its own fruit… for out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks."

— Luke 6:44–45 (ESV)

The fruit of our lives is set by what we believe in our hearts. And when we place our faith in something other than God, Scripture has a blunt name for it: we have "exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator" (Romans 1:25). That is idolatry — and sin, at its root, is unbelief (John 16:9).

So Nurture takes the fourth question and turns it personal. Instead of how do we live, we ask: What have we done? And then the question underneath it: what does that reveal about what we have been believing about God, and about ourselves?

We are a confessional people. We confess our beliefs, our unbelief, and the sin it produces — and in confessing to God and to each other, we find forgiveness and healing (James 5:16; 1 John 1:8–9). The working assumption of a DNA group is simply this: within any given week, every believer will sin. So every week there is something to confess, something to repent of, and grace waiting on the other side of it.

One warning. Nurture is not a witch-hunt, and it is not whack-a-mole. A group that turns into sin-detection will not survive the year, and it should not. We ought to be even quicker to name evidences of grace in one another than we are to name sin. The point is not to catch someone. The point is to get the truth about God into the place where the lie has been living.

"For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword… discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart."

— Hebrews 4:12 (ESV)

Act — The Hands

Once we have discovered what is true, confessed where we have not believed it, and turned back to Jesus in faith, there is one thing left: our repentance has to touch the actual week ahead.

John the Baptist told people to "bear fruit in keeping with repentance" — demonstrate by your life that this is real. Paul said the same: that people should "repent and turn to God, performing deeds in keeping with their repentance" (Acts 26:20). Legalism has left a sour taste for many of us, and understandably so, but the New Testament is not shy about calling for changed behaviour in light of the gospel.

"For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age."

— Titus 2:11–12 (ESV)

Notice what does the training there. Grace does. It is the experience of God's grace that makes us willing to say no to what is killing us and yes to what is good. Obedience that grows out of adoration and gratitude is a different animal from obedience that grows out of fear.

In practice this part is quiet. When the Spirit has led someone to repentance, the group spends five or ten minutes praying and listening, waiting on two questions:

  • What do you want me to do?
  • Who do you want me to tell?

Then it gets specific, in three directions: What will I do personally? What will we do together in our missional community? And what will we do missionally, among people who do not yet know Jesus? Because Jesus warned plainly against being a hearer of His word who never becomes a doer of it (Matthew 7:24–27).

Then You Pray It

The meeting ends by turning the whole hour into prayer — and the four questions map onto it exactly:

  • Adoration — for who God is.
  • Thanksgiving — for what He has done.
  • Confession — for what we have done, out of what we wrongly believed.
  • Supplication — asking the Spirit to empower what we will do.

Which is a good indication that the structure was never really a technique. It is just what happens when you take Scripture seriously in the presence of people who love you.

Why We Think This Is Integral

Here is the sentence that sits underneath all of it:

"Take care, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart, leading you to fall away from the living God. But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called 'today,' that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin."

— Hebrews 3:12–13 (ESV)

Read those two verses together and the logic is hard to escape. There is a real danger — a heart that stops believing and drifts. And there is a prescribed remedy — not a conference, not a curriculum, but each other, daily. Sin is described as deceitful, which is precisely the problem: a deceived person cannot detect their own deception. That is not a character flaw you can study your way out of. It requires someone else in the room.

Every single day we need reminding that we are deeply loved and fully accepted by the Father purely on the basis of what Jesus has done. That identity — sons and daughters, not employees — shapes everything else. It is what gives us both the boldness and the humility to be honest about our sin, because we are no longer defending a reputation we had to earn.

This is the same ground we walked in The Four Idols and Root to Fruit — that behind every sin is a lie about God, and that the fruit you can see is evidence of a root you usually cannot. If you have read those, you will recognise the four questions immediately. DNA is simply where that work stops being a concept and becomes a standing weekly appointment with two people who will not let you drift.

If You Are Thinking About Starting One

A few practical things worth knowing before you begin:

  • Pick your spot carefully. This conversation needs privacy. A living room or a quiet corner will serve you far better than a busy coffee shop.
  • Bring a Bible and something to write in. A journal through the week is where most of the discovering actually happens.
  • Bring a calendar. You will want to schedule time together beyond the meeting.
  • Leaders: pray for your people through the week, read ahead, and keep the discussion focused. And hand the leading around — the guide is not top secret, and a group where only one person can lead has a ceiling.

There is nothing magic about the acronym. What is powerful is three people, one open Bible, and enough honesty to say what is actually going on — every week, for long enough that it changes you.

If you would like to be part of one, or you are wondering whether to start one where you already are, we would love to help you find your two.
