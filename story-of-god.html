Discipleship Resource

The Story of God

One story, told in ten movements — from the first light of creation to a world made new.

The Big Idea

The Bible Is Not a Rulebook. It's a Story.

Most of us met the Bible in pieces — a verse on a coffee mug, a passage at a funeral, a lesson we half-remember from childhood. The pieces are true, but held apart from one another they rarely add up to anything we can live inside. The Story of God puts the pieces back in order.

Over ten sessions we walk the whole arc of Scripture — creation, rebellion, promise, rescue, Jesus, cross and resurrection, Spirit, church, and the restoration of everything — told as one continuous narrative. No lecture, no notes to fill in. Someone tells the story out loud, the group retells it together, and then we talk: What did you notice? What does this say about God? What does it say about us? Where do we see ourselves in it?

It is deliberately simple, because simple things travel. By the end, you don't just know about the Story — you can tell it. To a friend at work. To your kids at bedtime. To the neighbour who asks you an honest question over the fence. That's the point: not more information, but a story you can carry and hand on.

How It Works

A Session, Start to Finish

Roughly an hour together, following the same simple rhythm every time.

1

Review

Retell last week's story together before adding anything new. Nobody moves ahead until everyone is caught up.

2

Tell

The new story is told out loud — two to five minutes, straight through, no side trips or sermonizing.

3

Retell

The group puts the events back in order in their own words. This is where the Story starts to stick.

4

Dialogue

Open questions, honest answers, all of it anchored in the narrative itself — "Where did we see that in the story?"

5

Intersect

Connect your own story to God's. Where are you in this? What would change if it were true?

6

Repeat

Ten weeks, a weekend away, or a single long Saturday. Then turn around and lead it for someone else.

Ten Movements

The Symbols

Each session carries a simple symbol — a hook for the memory, so the whole Story can be recalled and retold on a napkin, a whiteboard, or the back of your hand. Tap any symbol to read its synopsis.

Session by Session

Walk Through the Story

A one-paragraph synopsis of each movement. These are summaries, not the sessions themselves — the Story is meant to be told out loud, in a room, with people you know.

Session One

Creation & Rebellion
Genesis 1–3 · Job 38 · Revelation 12

Before anything else, there was God — holy, good, and lacking nothing. He laid the foundations of the earth while angels sang for joy, and when some of those angels rebelled He would not let evil remain in His presence. Then He filled a world with light and water, seed and season, fish and birds and wild things, and called it good. Last of all He made humans in His own image, blessed them, shared His authority with them, and set them in a garden where everything they needed was already theirs. One tree was withheld — the knowledge of good and evil — because deciding what is good and what is evil belongs to God alone. Trusting a serpent's word over God's, Adam and Eve reached for it anyway, and the same rebellion that emptied heaven took root in the world. They were sent out of the garden, away from God's presence. And yet God came looking for them, and clothed them with His own hands before they went.

Session Two

Sinful Humanity
Genesis 4–9

Outside the garden the rebellion spreads. Cain, furious that his brother's offering was received and his was not, ignores God's warning that sin is crouching at the door — and kills Abel in a field. What began as one choice in one heart turns out to run through every heart. Generations later the earth is so saturated with violence that God grieves having made humanity at all, and the flood comes. But even in judgment God rescues: Noah, his family, and the animals ride out the ruin in a boat God told him to build. When the waters go down, Noah worships, God promises never to destroy the earth this way again, and hangs a rainbow in the clouds as the sign of it. This session sits with a hard truth — every one of us has chosen self over God, and God sees the heart, not just the behaviour — and with a stranger one: God keeps showing mercy to people who have not earned it.

Session Three

A People of God
Genesis 11–50

Humanity gathers on a plain to build a tower and make a name for itself; God confuses their language and scatters them across the earth. Then He does something nobody would have scripted — He sets out to bless the whole world through one family. He calls a childless seventy-five-year-old named Abram out of his country with a promise: a land, descendants beyond counting, and blessing for every nation on earth through his offspring. Abram believes God, and God counts it to him as righteousness. But the promise takes decades to arrive, and Abraham and Sarah stumble through it — impatience, a scheme with Hagar, laughter at God's timing — before Isaac is finally born. Isaac fathers Jacob, whose name God changes to Israel; Jacob's twelve sons become a nation, carried down into Egypt through Joseph and a famine. Every one of them is flawed. God chose them by grace, not merit, and He never once withdrew the promise. This is where our identity as the family of God begins.

Session Four

Exodus & Law
Exodus 1–20 · Leviticus · The Kings & Prophets

Four hundred years of slavery in Egypt, and God has not forgotten. He hears His people, raises up Moses, and confronts Pharaoh with plague after plague until the final night, when the blood of a lamb on the doorframe turns death aside and Israel walks out free. Pharaoh changes his mind and pursues them; God opens the sea, brings His people through on dry ground, and closes it behind them. Notice the order — rescue comes first, instruction second. At Sinai God gives Israel the Law, not to earn their freedom but to show a freed people how to live with a holy God in their midst, and He gives them a tabernacle, a priesthood, and a system of sacrifice so their sin can be atoned for and they can live close to Him. They were meant to display His glory to the watching nations. Instead the garden pattern repeats — judges, kings, prophets, exile, everyone doing what was right in their own eyes — and the Old Testament closes with a people still waiting on a promise God has not broken.

Session Five

The Person of Jesus
Matthew 1–4 · Luke 1–4 · John 1

After centuries of silence, God keeps His promise in person. Jesus is born to a young virgin in a backwater town — fully God, fully human — and grows up in obscurity working with His hands. At about thirty He walks down to the Jordan and asks John to baptize Him, stepping into the water shoulder to shoulder with sinful people though He had no sin of His own. The Spirit descends on Him like a dove, and the Father's voice says, "This is my Son, whom I love." Then that same Spirit drives Him into the wilderness, where for forty days the same tempter who spoke in the garden offers Him bread, safety, and power — and Jesus answers every offer with the word of God. Where the first humans grasped, He trusts. Where Israel failed, He obeys. Then He walks up to a handful of ordinary fishermen and a tax collector and says two words that reorder their lives: "Follow me."

Session Six

The Works of Jesus
The Gospels · Luke 15 · John 14

Jesus's life is a working preview of the world put right. He heals bodies nobody else would touch, calms a storm with a sentence, feeds thousands from a boy's lunch, drives out evil, and calls the dead back out of the grave — every miracle both a rescue for the person in front of Him and a sign of who He is. He teaches about the kingdom of God in stories that turn the room upside down: the last end up first, the lost get searched for until they're found, and a father hikes up his robe and runs down the road toward the son who wasted everything. He is the true King, and He arrives as a servant, saying plainly that He Himself is the way back to the Father. Then, on His last night, He takes bread and a cup with His friends, tells them His body will be broken and His blood poured out for the forgiveness of sins, and hands them a meal to remember it by until He comes back.

Session Seven

Death & Resurrection
Matthew 26–28 · Luke 22–24 · John 18–20

He is betrayed by a friend, arrested at night, and condemned on charges that will not hold — the governor says out loud that he finds no fault in Him. He is flogged, mocked, and crucified between two criminals, and while He is dying He prays for the people driving in the nails. At the moment of His death the temple curtain, the one that had stood between people and God's presence since Sinai, tears from top to bottom. This is the point the whole Story has been building toward: Jesus is the Passover Lamb and the final sacrifice, taking our sin and our punishment onto Himself, in our place, so we can be brought back. He is buried in a borrowed tomb, sealed with a stone and guarded by soldiers. On the third day the stone is rolled away and the tomb is empty — not stolen, not symbolic. He is alive, and He shows Himself, over and over, to people who touch Him and eat with Him. Sin, death, and evil are beaten. Jesus is Lord over all of it.

Session Eight

The Mission & Power
John 20 · Matthew 28 · Acts 1–2 · 1 Corinthians 15

Over forty days the risen Jesus appears to more than five hundred witnesses — and He is patient with doubt, telling Thomas to put his hand in the wound and see for himself. Then He gives His followers their commission: all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Him, so go and make disciples of every nation, baptizing them and teaching them to walk in His ways, and know that He is with them always, to the very end. And with that He ascends. Days later a hundred and twenty of them are praying together in Jerusalem when a sound like a roaring wind fills the house, the Holy Spirit fills every one of them, and they spill out into the street speaking languages they never learned. A crowd gathers, and Peter — the one who denied Him — stands up and explains: this is the promise the prophets spoke of, Jesus is the Messiah, turn from your sins and find forgiveness in Him. Three thousand believe and are baptized that day. The mission belongs to Jesus; the power belongs to His Spirit.

Session Nine

Life in the Spirit
Acts 2 & 4 · Acts 9 · Galatians 5 · 2 Corinthians 8

So what do Spirit-filled people actually do on a Tuesday? Luke tells us plainly. They devote themselves to the apostles' teaching, to prayer, to breaking bread in one another's homes, and to each other — so thoroughly that there is not a needy person among them, because the ones who own land and houses sell them to cover the ones who don't. They eat together with glad and generous hearts, celebrate communion, testify to the resurrection, and God adds to their number daily. Guided by the Spirit the church spills out past Jerusalem, and Jesus even reaches into the ranks of His enemies and calls Paul — who had been hunting Christians — to carry the good news across the Roman world, planting churches and writing letters to keep them faithful on mission in their own cities. This is ordinary life reordered by the Spirit: character reshaped from the inside out, held in community, spent on mission. It is the life we're inviting you into.

Session Ten

Creation Restored
Luke 17 · Acts 3 · 1 Corinthians 15 · Revelation 21–22

Jesus promised to come back, and He said that when He does nobody will need to be told — it will be like lightning flashing across the whole sky. He will set creation free from its curse, put an end to evil and sin and rebellion for good, and raise His people to live forever in bodies like His own. God gave John a glimpse of it and John wrote it down: a new heaven and a new earth, the holy city coming down out of heaven dressed like a bride, a river of the water of life running clear as crystal down the middle of the main street, and the Tree of Life growing on both banks with leaves for the healing of the nations. God Himself will live with His people, and He will wipe every tear from their eyes — no more death, no more grief, no more crying, no more pain, because the old order of things has passed away. The Story ends where it started, only better: God with us, at home, in a world made new.

Three Ways In

How to Walk Through It

Ten Weeks

One session a week with your missional community. The slowest and, honestly, the best — the Story has time to settle into the ordinary rhythms of your life together.

A Weekend

Get away together Friday to Sunday. A powerful way to launch a new community or DNA group, with real time to talk between sessions.

One Day

Eight in the morning to five at night, breaks and a shared lunch included. Ideal for a core team, or when childcare is easier to arrange for one full day.
